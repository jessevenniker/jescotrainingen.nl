The licences have been bought, the demo has been given, and yet a large part of the team barely touches the new AI tool. Sound familiar? Then the problem rarely lies in the technology. It lies in how people see the tool, and in what they need to use it well.

## What research says about acceptance

Since the 1980s, researchers have studied why people do or don't take up new technology. The best-known model named two factors: expected usefulness ("does this help me?") and ease of use ("can I do this?") (Davis, 1989).

Later models added two important things: social influence (what are colleagues and managers doing?) and facilitating conditions (do I have the training, support and resources I need?) (Venkatesh et al., 2003). An extension of that model showed that habit and enjoyment play a part too (Venkatesh et al., 2012).

Applied to AI at work, three more questions come up in practice: can I trust the output, do I get a say in how I use it, and what does it mean for my job?

## Why having a say weighs so heavily

According to self-determination theory, people are more motivated when they experience three things: autonomy, a sense of competence, and connection with others (Deci & Ryan, 2000).

AI imposed from above can put all three under pressure. People feel less in control, doubt their own ability and feel they have to figure it out alone. AI that people can choose to use for tasks they pick themselves, while learning from each other, strengthens all three instead.

## Four kinds of employee

If you look at attitude (do I see the value?) and ability (can I do it, and do I have what I need?), you'll often see four groups in practice:

- **Front-runners** see the value and can work with it. Watch out: they often do the most AI work, which puts them at greater risk of overload.
- **Starters** are keen, but it isn't working yet. They mainly need training and examples.
- **Critical professionals** can do it, but have reservations. Their sharpness is valuable when agreeing how output gets checked.
- **People at a distance** feel unsure or pressured. With them, you start with a conversation, not a tool.

## What your team can do

1. **Start with the why.** Be honest about why you're bringing in AI and what it means for roles and tasks.
2. **Let teams choose** which tasks AI adds value to. That gives people a say and leads to better choices.
3. **Invest in training and agreements**, not just licences. Ease of use and practical conditions matter as much as the tool itself.
4. **Make front-runners visible**, but give them time for it. Seeing how a colleague does it works better than a manual.
5. **Look beyond usage**, at workload and how people feel too. Lots of use isn't the same as good use.

## A caveat

The acceptance models are well researched, but mostly for earlier technology such as software and information systems. Generative AI differs in a few ways: its output is less predictable and it touches more directly on people's craft. The factors remain useful, but their weight may differ from team to team and from task to task.

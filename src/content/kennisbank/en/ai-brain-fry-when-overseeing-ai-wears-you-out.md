AI is doing more and more on its own. With the latest features, you can have an agent carry out a series of tasks, run a piece of research or sort your inbox. That sounds like less work. But your job does change: from doing the work to watching, judging and correcting. And that turns out to be surprisingly hard.

## A new term: AI brain fry

In March 2026, researchers at Boston Consulting Group published a survey of 1,488 full-time US workers in Harvard Business Review (Bedard et al., 2026). They describe something they call *AI brain fry*: mental fatigue from using or overseeing AI tools beyond what your brain can handle.

Participants described a kind of fog or buzzing in their heads, trouble concentrating, slower decisions and sometimes headaches. Some genuinely had to step away from their screens.

## Oversight is the heaviest part

The most striking finding: it wasn't using AI as such that drained people most, but **overseeing** it. People who had to check a lot of AI work reported 14 percent more mental effort, 12 percent more mental fatigue and 19 percent more information overload.

The number of tools mattered too. Up to about three AI tools, people felt more productive. At four or more, that sense of productivity dropped sharply.

And it has consequences. Among workers experiencing brain fry, 34 percent intended to leave their job, compared with 25 percent of those who didn't.

## Not burnout, but a warning sign

Brain fry isn't the same as burnout. It's acute: it flares up during intense AI work and fades when you take a break. Burnout builds slowly and doesn't go away after a coffee.

Still, it's a signal worth taking seriously. Long before generative AI, research on technostress showed that technology demanding more, faster and more complex work goes hand in hand with more stress and lower productivity (Tarafdar et al., 2007). And in a study by the Upwork Research Institute (2024), 77 percent of employees who use AI said their workload had gone up.

## What helps

The researchers found that brain fry was less common among people who got proper training and support from their manager. Beyond that, a few simple agreements help:

1. **Fewer tools, used well.** Agree on a handful of tools that genuinely fit, rather than a different one for everything.
2. **Set times for reviewing.** Check AI output in blocks rather than all day in between other work.
3. **Know what you're checking.** Agree which output needs a thorough review and which doesn't. Not everything matters equally.
4. **Pause before hard decisions.** Step back after a block of AI work, especially before deciding something important.
5. **Redesign, don't pile on.** Don't stack AI on top of existing work; look together at which tasks can genuinely change.

## A caveat

This is a US survey run by a consultancy and published in a business magazine, not a peer-reviewed journal. People reported for themselves how they felt. The concept is new and deserves more research. But the pattern will feel familiar to many teams, especially now that AI is doing more and more on its own.
